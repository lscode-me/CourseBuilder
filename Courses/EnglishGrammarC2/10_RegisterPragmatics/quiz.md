## Answer Key

| # | Answer | Explanation |
|---|--------|-------------|
| 1 | D | "Interesting" from a manager is often damning with faint praise—signals doubt. |
| 2 | C | Negative face = freedom from imposition; requests impose on this. |
| 3 | D | Direct imperative < conditional < past modal with hedging (most polite). |
| 4 | B | Understatement: "not bad" typically means "good." |
| 5 | C | Social formula—often said without genuine intention to arrange lunch. |
| 6 | B | "Dear Mr./Ms. [Name]" is appropriate formal business register. |
| 7 | A | "I wonder if..." is diplomatic; direct disagreement is impolite. |
| 8 | A | Russian communication style transfers as too direct in English. |
| 9 | C | "With the greatest respect" typically precedes disagreement or criticism. |
| 10 | D | Formal writing uses full forms and avoids contractions and phrasal verbs. |
| 11 | B | "Just" and "possibly" are hedges that soften the request. |
| 12 | B | "I'm afraid that might be difficult" is diplomatic refusal language. |
| 13 | D | Accept compliments gracefully in English; deflecting seems odd. |
| 14 | D | Usually a polite way to dismiss a suggestion without committing. |
| 15 | B | British English is generally more indirect than American. |
| 16 | B | Statement functions as indirect request for action. |
| 17 | D | "Kind regards" is appropriately professional but not stiff. |
| 18 | D | Small talk is expected and valued in English business culture. |
| 19 | B | Self-deprecation is common; minimizing achievements shows modesty. |
| 20 | C | Diplomatic way to say there are problems; not actually praise. |
