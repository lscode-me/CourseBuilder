## 1. Two Types of Modality

### 1.1 Epistemic vs Deontic

Модальные глаголы имеют **два типа значений**:

**DEONTIC (деонтическое):** Об обязательствах, разрешениях, способностях
```
"You must wear a seatbelt." (obligation)
"You can leave now." (permission)
"She can speak French." (ability)
```

**EPISTEMIC (эпистемическое):** О вероятности, логических выводах
```
"It must be cold outside." (logical deduction)
"He might be at home." (possibility)
"She can't be serious." (impossibility)
```

### 1.2 Why It Matters

The same modal can mean different things:

```
"She must work hard."
→ Deontic: She is obliged to work hard (external requirement)
→ Epistemic: She probably works hard (my conclusion)

"You can't go."
→ Deontic: You're not allowed to go (no permission)
→ Epistemic: It's impossible that you go (doesn't make sense)
```

Context determines the meaning!

## 2. Possibility and Probability

### 2.1 The Certainty Scale

From most certain to least certain:

```
CERTAIN:        must / can't (negative)
PROBABLE:       should / ought to
POSSIBLE:       may / might / could
UNCERTAIN:      might not / may not
IMPOSSIBLE:     can't / couldn't
```

### 2.2 Examples

**Must (logical certainty):**
```
"She's not answering. She must be asleep."
"He's worked here for 20 years. He must know the system well."
```

**Should / Ought to (expectation):**
```
"The train should arrive at 6." (expected)
"The package ought to be here by now." (expected)
```

**May / Might / Could (possibility):**
```
"He may be right." (≈ 50% chance)
"It might rain later." (possibility)
"She could be at work." (one possibility among many)
```

**Can't / Couldn't (impossibility):**
```
"That can't be true!" (I don't believe it)
"She couldn't have said that." (impossible, surely)
```

### 2.3 May vs Might vs Could

All three express possibility, but with subtle differences:

```
MAY: More likely, more formal
"It may rain tomorrow." (I think there's a good chance)

MIGHT: Less likely, less formal
"It might rain." (but probably won't)

COULD: One of several possibilities
"She could be at home, at work, or at the gym."
```

In practice, might and could are often interchangeable.

## 3. Obligation and Necessity

### 3.1 Must vs Have to

Both express obligation, but:

**MUST:** Speaker's opinion, internal obligation
```
"I must call my mother." (I feel I should)
"You must try this cake!" (strong recommendation)
"I must stop smoking." (personal decision)
```

**HAVE TO:** External obligation, rules
```
"I have to call my mother." (she told me to)
"You have to wear a helmet." (it's the law)
"I have to stop smoking." (doctor's orders)
```

In **questions**, "have to" is more common:
```
"Do I have to come?" (more natural)
"Must I come?" (formal, rare)
```

### 3.2 Negative: Don't Have To vs Mustn't

**CRUCIAL DIFFERENCE:**

```
DON'T HAVE TO = no obligation (optional)
"You don't have to come." (it's optional)

MUSTN'T = prohibition (not allowed)
"You mustn't smoke here." (it's forbidden)
```

**Russian speakers' error:** Confusing these because Russian "не должен" can mean both.

### 3.3 Should vs Ought to

Both express advice/recommendation:

```
"You should see a doctor." (advice)
"You ought to see a doctor." (slightly more formal)
```

In modern English, "should" is much more common.

### 3.4 Need

"Need" can be modal or regular verb:

```
MODAL (formal/negative/question):
"Need I say more?"
"You needn't worry."

REGULAR VERB (more common):
"Do I need to say more?"
"You don't need to worry."
```

## 4. Permission and Requests

### 4.1 Permission Scale (giving/asking)

From informal to formal:

```
INFORMAL:                    FORMAL:
Can I...?                    Could I possibly...?
Can you...?                  Would you mind...?
Could I...?                  I wonder if I could...?
May I...?                    Would it be possible to...?
```

### 4.2 Can vs Could vs May

**CAN:** Informal, direct
```
"Can I use your phone?"
"You can sit here."
```

**COULD:** More polite, conditional
```
"Could I ask a question?"
"Could you help me?"
```

**MAY:** Formal, especially for permission
```
"May I come in?" (formal)
"You may leave." (giving permission formally)
```

### 4.3 Request Structures

**C1 speakers use varied request forms:**

```
DIRECT (least polite):
"Open the window."

WITH MODAL:
"Can you open the window?"
"Could you open the window?"
"Would you open the window?"
"Will you open the window?"

INDIRECT (most polite):
"Would you mind opening the window?"
"I wonder if you could open the window."
"Would it be possible to open the window?"
"I don't suppose you could open the window?"
```

**Russian speakers' error:** Using only "can you" for all situations. Vary your requests!

## 5. Hedging: Academic and Professional English

### 5.1 What is Hedging?

**Hedging** = making statements less direct, certain, or absolute. Essential in academic writing and professional communication.

```
WITHOUT HEDGING:
"This causes cancer."

WITH HEDGING:
"This may cause cancer."
"This could potentially contribute to cancer."
"This appears to be linked to cancer."
```

### 5.2 Hedging with Modals

**Academic hedging:**
```
"This might suggest that..."
"The data could indicate..."
"These results may support the hypothesis..."
"This would seem to confirm..."
```

**Professional hedging:**
```
"We might need to reconsider..."
"I would suggest that..."
"Perhaps we could try..."
"It might be worth exploring..."
```

### 5.3 Would for Tentative Statements

"Would" makes statements less assertive:

```
DIRECT: "I think we should change the strategy."
HEDGED: "I would think we should change the strategy."

DIRECT: "This is incorrect."
HEDGED: "This would appear to be incorrect."
```

### 5.4 Combinations

Combine hedging devices:

```
"It might perhaps be argued that..."
"This would seem to suggest that..."
"One could potentially interpret this as..."
```

## 6. Modals + Perfect Infinitive

### 6.1 Talking About the Past

Modal + have + past participle = past reference

```
"He must have left." (certainly left)
"She may have forgotten." (possibly forgot)
"They could have been delayed." (possibly were)
"You should have called." (but you didn't)
```

### 6.2 Deductions About the Past

**Must have:**
```
"He's not here. He must have gone home."
(Logical conclusion about past)
```

**Can't have / Couldn't have:**
```
"She can't have said that. It's not like her."
(Impossible, I don't believe it)
```

**May/Might/Could have:**
```
"She might have missed the train."
(Past possibility)
```

### 6.3 Criticisms and Regrets

**Should have / Ought to have (criticism/regret):**
```
"You should have told me." (but you didn't)
"I ought to have studied harder." (but I didn't)
"We shouldn't have spent so much." (but we did)
```

**Could have (missed opportunity):**
```
"You could have helped me!" (you had the ability but didn't)
"We could have won." (we had the chance but didn't)
```

**Needn't have (unnecessary action):**
```
"You needn't have bought a gift." (you did, but it wasn't necessary)
vs.
"You didn't need to buy a gift." (and maybe you didn't)
```

### 6.4 Would have (unreal past)

```
"I would have helped if you had asked."
(Conditional — you didn't ask, so I didn't help)

"I would have thought so."
(Tentative statement about expectation)
```

## 7. Specific Modal Meanings

### 7.1 Will / Would

**Will:**
```
Prediction: "It will rain tomorrow."
Promise: "I'll call you later."
Offer: "I'll help you with that."
Refusal: "He won't listen to me."
Characteristic: "She'll often work late."
```

**Would:**
```
Conditional: "I would help if I could."
Past habit: "We would often go fishing."
Polite request: "Would you sign here?"
Hypothetical: "It would be great if..."
Willingness: "She wouldn't agree to the plan."
```

### 7.2 Can / Could

**Can:**
```
Ability: "I can swim."
Permission: "You can go."
Request: "Can you help?"
Possibility: "It can be dangerous."
```

**Could:**
```
Past ability: "I could swim when I was 5."
Polite request: "Could you help?"
Possibility: "It could rain."
Conditional: "I could help if..."
```

**Note:** For past single achievement, use "was able to":
```
"After trying for hours, I was able to fix it." (specific success)
NOT: "I could fix it." (general ability)
```

### 7.3 Shall

Mostly British, declining in use:

```
Offers: "Shall I help you?"
Suggestions: "Shall we go?"
Formal/legal: "The tenant shall pay..."
```

### 7.4 Used to

**Not a true modal** but often grouped with them:

```
Past habit: "I used to smoke."
Past state: "This used to be a forest."
Question: "Did you use to live here?"
Negative: "I didn't use to like coffee."
```

## 8. Common Errors by Russian Speakers

### 8.1 Infinitive with "To"

```
❌ "I must to go."
✅ "I must go."

❌ "She can to swim."
✅ "She can swim."

❌ "You should to try."
✅ "You should try."
```

**Why:** Russian "должен/могу + infinitive" often requires explicit marker. English modals don't take "to."

### 8.2 Modal + -s

```
❌ "She cans swim."
✅ "She can swim."

❌ "He musts leave."
✅ "He must leave."
```

Modals don't change for person!

### 8.3 Double Modals

```
❌ "I will can come."
✅ "I will be able to come."

❌ "You must can see him."
✅ "You must be able to see him."
```

English doesn't allow two modals together (unlike some dialects).

### 8.4 Mustn't vs Don't Have To

```
RUSSIAN: "Ты не должен" can mean either

ENGLISH:
"You mustn't do that." (prohibition — forbidden!)
"You don't have to do that." (no obligation — optional)
```

### 8.5 Can vs Could for Past

```
❌ "Yesterday I could buy the tickets." (specific success)
✅ "Yesterday I was able to buy the tickets."
✅ "Yesterday I managed to buy the tickets."

BUT:
✅ "When I was young, I could run fast." (general past ability)
```

### 8.6 Overuse of Must

Russian speakers often overuse "must" where "should" or "have to" would be better:

```
❌ "You must see this film!" (sounds like an order)
✅ "You should see this film!" / "You have to see this film!"

❌ "I must go to work at 9." (external rule → have to)
✅ "I have to go to work at 9."
```

## 9. Politeness Strategies

### 9.1 Increasing Politeness

**Step by step from direct to indirect:**

```
Level 1: "Give me the report."
Level 2: "Give me the report, please."
Level 3: "Can you give me the report?"
Level 4: "Could you give me the report?"
Level 5: "Could you possibly give me the report?"
Level 6: "Would you mind giving me the report?"
Level 7: "I was wondering if you could give me the report."
Level 8: "I don't suppose you could give me the report?"
```

### 9.2 Professional Email Examples

```
Too direct:
"Send me the file."

Better:
"Could you send me the file?"

Formal:
"I would appreciate it if you could send me the file."

Very polite:
"I wonder if you might be able to send me the file when you have a moment."
```

### 9.3 Softening Bad News

```
Direct: "That's wrong."
Softer: "That might not be quite right."

Direct: "I disagree."
Softer: "I'm not sure I would agree with that."

Direct: "You made a mistake."
Softer: "There may have been a small oversight."
```

## 💡 Key Takeaways

- **Epistemic vs Deontic**: Same modal, different meanings (probability vs obligation)
- **Politeness gradient**: Vary modals based on social context
- **Hedging is essential**: Use may/might/could/would in academic and professional writing
- **Modal + perfect**: For past — must have, should have, could have
- **No "to" after modals**: Unlike Russian, English modals are directly followed by base verb
