## Question 1: Epistemic vs Deontic

A) Obligation — she is required to be tired

B) Logical deduction — I conclude she is tired

C) Permission — she is allowed to be tired

D) Ability — she has the capacity to be tired

## Answer Key

| # | Answer | Explanation |
|---|--------|-------------|
| 1 | B | This is epistemic "must" — expressing logical deduction/certainty, not obligation. |
| 2 | C | "Must" expresses near-certainty (logical deduction). Might/could/may express lower probability. |
| 3 | B | "Have to" is typically used for external rules/obligations. "Must" often expresses personal/internal obligation. |
| 4 | B | "Don't have to" = no obligation (you can choose). "Mustn't" = prohibition (forbidden). Very different! |
| 5 | C | "Would you mind + -ing" is more polite than direct requests or "can/could you." |
| 6 | C | "May contribute to" is hedged — cautious, appropriate for academic writing. The others are too certain. |
| 7 | B | Should have + past participle expresses criticism about past action that didn't happen. |
| 8 | B | Modal + have + past participle. "Must have left" — deduction about past. |
| 9 | B | For specific past achievements, use "was able to" or "managed to," not "could." |
| 10 | C | "Must to call" — Russian speakers often add "to" after modals. Correct: "must call." |
| 11 | B | Modals don't take -s for third person. "He must" not "He musts." |
| 12 | A | "Could have" expresses unrealized past possibility, often as reproach — you had the ability but didn't act. |
| 13 | B | "Needn't have" = you did something that turned out to be unnecessary. You brought the gift! |
| 14 | B | "Would you + infinitive" is a polite request form. The others are conditional, past habit, hypothetical. |
| 15 | B | "Shall I...?" is an offer or suggestion to do something. Common in British English. |
| 16 | B | "May" is more formal than "might" and often suggests slightly higher probability. |
| 17 | B | "Would" here softens/hedges the opinion, making it less direct and more tentative. |
| 18 | C | "Can't have + past participle" expresses disbelief — the speaker considers it impossible. |
| 19 | C | Two modal verbs together ("will can") is not allowed. Use "will be able to." |
| 20 | C | The most hedged/softened version. Uses indirect expression instead of direct "wrong." |
